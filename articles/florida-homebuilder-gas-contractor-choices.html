Building a Home in Florida? Know Your Builder and Your Choices

Can I use my own gas guy? Understand your choices, ask better questions, and bring your gas specialist into the plan early.

By Guardian Gas Solutions LLC · Reviewed November 10, 2026

Your new home involves more than floor plans and finishes. It also involves deciding who will build the systems you will depend on every day—and understanding who controls those decisions.

A capable builder brings planning, supervision, and coordination to a complicated project. Homeowners bring the budget, priorities, and expectations. A strong working relationship makes those responsibilities clear before construction begins.

One question deserves a place near the top of your list:

“Can I choose my own licensed gas contractor?”

Ask it before signing. The answer can affect your installation, construction schedule, warranty arrangements, and future propane service.

First, understand what “GC” means in Florida

People often use “GC” to describe any homebuilder. Florida distinguishes several license categories.

Contractor category General scope
General contractor Broad authority over construction projects, subject to statutory restrictions and requirements for specific trades.
Building contractor Commercial and residential buildings up to three stories, with associated accessory structures; also certain work on larger buildings when structural members are unaffected.
Residential contractor One-, two-, and three-family residences with up to two habitable stories above no more than one uninhabitable story, plus associated accessory structures.

These are summaries of Florida Statutes §489.105, not substitutes for checking the proposed work against the license.

For these construction licenses, certified generally means statewide authority within the license’s scope. Registered means authority within the jurisdictions covered by that registration. A broader license does not, by itself, establish better workmanship or project management.

Florida also requires appropriate credentials for specialized work performed by electricians, mechanical contractors, gas fitters and many others. A GC license does not automatically authorize every specialized installation.

Verify the license, its status, and the business it qualifies. Gas work deserves its own credential check.

“Can I use my own gas guy?”

You can ask to select your gas contractor. Whether the builder must accept that selection depends on the agreement and circumstances.

Treat this as a contract question to resolve early. Owning the home or lot does not, by itself, settle who may enter an active construction site or perform work under the builder’s project.

A custom builder may accept an owner-requested specialist. A production builder may offer a more restricted selection process. Ask each builder what is available before choosing one.

Start with:

“I would like Guardian Gas Solutions LLC to handle my gas installation. What requirements must they meet, and can we put that arrangement in the contract?”

Then establish how the arrangement will work:

Possible arrangement What to clarify
Your preferred gas contractor becomes the builder’s subcontractor Who contracts, pays, schedules, supervises, and handles warranty calls?
You hire the gas contractor directly, with written builder approval Who controls site access, coordinates permits and inspections, and resolves overlapping responsibilities?
The builder requires its existing gas subcontractor What does the proposal include, who performs the work, and are alternatives available before you sign?

A builder may reasonably review licensing, insurance, availability, and coordination requirements. Ask for the actual requirements and any fees in writing.

If you already signed a contract, request an approved written change before hiring someone to work on the site.

Questions that make “yes” meaningful

A verbal “sure” leaves too much unresolved. Ask:

  • Will you approve my preferred gas contractor in writing?
  • Must that contractor work through you, or may I contract directly?
  • What credit removes the gas work already included in your price?
  • Will you charge coordination, supervision, administration, or markup fees?
  • Who handles permits, inspections, corrections, and return visits?
  • When must the gas contractor complete each stage?
  • Who pays if the site is unready or another trade causes damage?
  • Who handles warranty service, and how are responsibilities divided?

If the answer is “no,” ask whether the reason is contractual, insurance-related, scheduling-related, or a company policy. Understanding the reason helps you evaluate your options.

Plumber or gas fitter? Ask who installs gas piping every day

“Who will install my gas piping—and is fuel-gas installation a regular part of that person's working day?”

That question deserves more than “our plumber handles it.” Ask about the actual crew's experience.

Plumbing is a broad trade. Florida's plumbing-contractor scope includes water, drainage, and specified gas work. Some plumbing companies also operate experienced gas crews. A dedicated gas-fitting business concentrates on fuel-gas systems. These descriptions can overlap: a plumber may also be a highly experienced gas specialist. The words “gas fitter” alone do not establish a Florida license or prove competence. [Sources: Florida Statutes §489.105(3)(m); FDACS LP Gas Licenses.]

Legal authorization and daily specialization are different questions. Verify both.

A plumbing credential does not tell you how often the assigned crew installs gas systems. A gas-focused company should likewise be able to demonstrate its credentials, recent comparable work, training, and supervision. Ask about the people coming to your property, not just the person whose name appears on the proposal.

Gas piping deserves focused attention

Your fuel-gas piping carries combustible fuel. Escaping propane or natural gas can create a fire or explosion hazard when mixed with air and exposed to an ignition source. That is why gas installation deserves deliberate planning and qualified workmanship. [Source: PHMSA, Safety Regulation for Small LPG Distribution Systems.]

Gas work should receive a specialist's attention from planning through final connection. It should never become an afterthought attached to the plumbing package.

Ask the proposed installer to explain who is responsible for evaluating appliance demand, pipe sizing, pressure regulation, material selection, required testing and inspections, appliance connections, and completion records. Confirm which of those services are actually included in the proposal.

A contractor who regularly performs comparable gas installations should be able to discuss that scope clearly and show relevant completed projects. Frequency alone is not a guarantee of quality; it is one useful part of checking experience.

Questions to put directly to your builder

  • Does your plumbing subcontractor have a dedicated gas crew, or is gas an occasional assignment?
  • How often does the actual installer complete propane or natural gas systems like mine?
  • What licenses, training, and supervision apply to that crew and this scope?
  • Who checks system capacity when my appliance selections change?
  • Who completes testing, final connections, and any agreed startup work?
  • Who returns if there is a problem after move-in?
  • May I choose a qualified gas specialist whose everyday work matches this installation?

You can respect the builder's plumbing team while asking for a gas specialist. Your goal is to select people with demonstrated experience in the system they will install in your home.

Your gas contractor should be part of planning

Gas work should enter the conversation while plans and appliance choices are still being developed.

Ask your gas professional to review the intended appliances, fuel type, installation scope, and future plans with the builder and other relevant professionals.

That conversation might include a range, tankless water heater, fireplace, dryer, pool heater, outdoor kitchen, or generator. A future appliance should be discussed as a future design requirement; an unused connection alone does not establish capacity.

Ask:

  • Who confirms the appliance models and fuel requirements?
  • Who evaluates the proposed system for the intended demand?
  • Which items include final connections, conversions, testing, or startup?
  • Who coordinates tank or meter location with the site plan?
  • What happens if an appliance changes after the design is approved?
  • What documentation will I receive at completion?

These questions help prevent scope gaps—for example, discovering that a proposal includes piping but leaves appliance connections to someone else.

Verify the gas credentials separately

Florida’s Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services administers LP gas licenses. Its Category V installer license covers specified installation, service, design, modification, and repair activities involving LP gas equipment, piping, appliances, and tanks, and requires a designated master qualifier.

Ask which credentials authorize the actual work proposed. For natural gas, verify the applicable license scope and utility requirements rather than assuming every gas-related credential covers every activity.

The propane tank: who owns it after closing?

“Your house comes with a propane tank” is not a complete explanation.

Ask whether the tank will be purchased, leased, or supplied under another agreement—and request the documents before installation.

Some supplier-owned tank agreements restrict who may fill the tank or work on the supplier’s equipment. For example, Florida Public Utilities’ published propane terms contain supplier-exclusive filling provisions for company-owned tanks. Your actual agreement controls.

Ask:

  • Who owns the tank, regulators, and other equipment?
  • If I am buying the tank, what document confirms ownership?
  • If it is leased, what rental, minimum-purchase, termination, or removal terms apply?
  • May I choose my propane supplier?
  • Who provides the first fill, and is it included?
  • Who handles maintenance and future repairs?
  • What happens when I sell the home?

Choosing the installer, owning the tank, and choosing the fuel supplier are separate decisions. Get an answer to each.

Evaluate experience through completed work

A recently licensed builder may bring substantial prior construction experience, strong mentorship, and excellent organization. Another may still be developing the skills needed to run an entire project.

An established builder may offer deep judgment and dependable trade relationships. Another may struggle with workload, documentation, or communication.

Avoid using age or license date as a shortcut. Ask for evidence:

  • How many comparable homes have you completed under this business?
  • What was your personal role?
  • Who will supervise my home day to day?
  • May I speak with a recent client and a client whose home has been occupied for a year?
  • Can you describe a significant problem and how you resolved it?

Compare both construction experience and management capacity.

AI, software, and the person accountable for your project

Builders use different tools: project portals, scheduling software, spreadsheets, AI assistance, and established office routines.

Technology adoption alone does not establish competence. A homeowner needs accurate information and someone responsible for acting on it.

Ask the builder to show how a selection or change reaches the field:

  • Where is the current approved plan kept?
  • How do subcontractors receive revisions?
  • Who confirms they received and understood them?
  • Who checks AI-assisted schedules, estimates, or communications?
  • Can I see a sample weekly progress report?
  • What happens when the office system and site conditions disagree?

Evaluate the result: current documents, acknowledged changes, realistic schedules, and clear follow-through.

Does the builder have enough support to keep work moving?

A small builder can deliver excellent work. A larger staff can provide useful coverage. The question is whether the support fits the workload.

Ask who handles supervision, purchasing, permits, inspections, accounting, and homeowner communication. Then ask who covers each responsibility when that person is unavailable.

Useful questions include:

  • How many homes will my superintendent manage?
  • Which upcoming trades are confirmed?
  • Who schedules the gas rough-in and final connections?
  • What happens if a key subcontractor becomes unavailable?
  • How quickly will you notify me of a delay?
  • Will I receive a revised schedule and explanation?

A planned wait for an inspection differs from an unexplained stoppage. Ask for the reason, responsible person, and recovery plan.

What homeowners often discover too late

An allowance is a budget placeholder. Ask what happens when your selections exceed it.

Removing work may not remove the entire listed amount. Negotiate the credit and any remaining fees before approving a separate trade.

A friendly promise needs documentation. Record the scope, price, schedule effect, and approval process for changes.

Warranty responsibility needs specific answers. Florida law provides a mandatory builder warranty for certain defects involving material building-code violations, with defined limits and exclusions. Owner-directed work can affect coverage for defects it causes. Ask for a precise explanation rather than accepting a blanket statement that choosing a trade “voids everything.”

Paying the builder does not necessarily eliminate lien exposure. Certain unpaid subcontractors and suppliers may have lien rights even after you pay the builder. Have a proper process for notices, payment documentation, releases, and final-payment requirements.

Your choices are strongest before you sign

Before committing, verify the builder’s credentials and insurance, compare comparable completed projects, and review the written scope and payment terms.

Put your preferred gas contractor on the agenda alongside your floor plan and finishes. Establish who contracts with that specialist, what the builder charges, and how the work fits into the schedule.

If work later stops or a dispute develops, preserve the contract, payment records, photographs, inspection history, and communications. Obtain advice before terminating the builder, withholding disputed payments, or hiring a replacement. Florida’s contractor-payment statute includes permit and commencement requirements for certain residential deposits, with exceptions and specific written-demand procedures.

You do not need to become a construction expert to participate meaningfully. You need clear answers, written agreements, and a team willing to explain its work.

Planning gas for your Florida home?

Tell your builder early:

“I want to discuss using Guardian Gas Solutions LLC for my gas installation. Please explain the approval process, scope, fees, and coordination requirements before we finalize the contract.”
