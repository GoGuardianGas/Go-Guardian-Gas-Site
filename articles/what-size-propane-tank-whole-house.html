What Size Propane Tank Do I Need for a Whole House?

Tank size isn't based only on gallons or home size. It's determined by total energy demand, how appliances operate, vaporization rate, and future needs. Here's how sizing really works.

One of the most common questions homeowners ask when considering propane is: what size propane tank do I need for my whole house? It seems simple — many people assume the answer is based only on the number of appliances or the size of the home. The reality is different. A properly sized tank is determined by understanding the total energy demand of the home, how the appliances operate, expected usage, delivery requirements, and future needs. A propane tank isn't just a container that holds fuel — it's the foundation of an energy system designed to safely and reliably supply every connected appliance.

The First Question: What Appliances Will Propane Supply?

Sizing begins with identifying every appliance connected to the system — gas ranges and cooktops, clothes dryers, fireplaces, tankless water heaters, furnaces, pool heaters, outdoor kitchens, fire features like fire pits and gas lanterns, and generators. Each has a BTU rating. As rough figures: a range runs about 50,000–75,000 BTU, a dryer 20,000–35,000, a fireplace 30,000–50,000, a tankless water heater 160,000–199,000, a pool heater around 399,900, and a standby generator 200,000–600,000+. The total demand determines the required system capacity.

Common Whole-Home Propane Tank Sizes

120-Gallon Tank

Often used for cooking only, small fireplaces, and limited appliance loads. Usually not considered a whole-home solution for higher-demand applications.

250-Gallon Tank

Common for smaller homes — cooking, dryers, fireplaces, and limited hot water. Works for moderate usage but may be undersized for homes with large appliances or generators.

500-Gallon Tank

One of the most common whole-home residential tanks. Often used for multiple appliances, tankless water heaters, fireplaces, pool equipment, and residential generators. It provides a good balance between capacity, installation requirements, and delivery frequency.

1,000-Gallon Tank

Typically for larger homes, multiple high-demand appliances, large pool heaters, whole-home generators, and estates with significant usage. A larger tank reduces delivery frequency and provides greater reserve, especially when extended duration of use is expected.

Why Tank Size Isn't Based Only on Gallons

A common misconception is that more gallons always means better. The tank must also provide the required vaporization rate. Propane changes from liquid to vapor inside the tank, and during high demand the tank has to produce enough vapor to supply the connected appliances. Vaporization is affected by tank size, outdoor temperature, fill level, appliance demand, and installation location. A tank may physically hold enough fuel but still struggle to supply enough vapor during peak demand.

What About a Whole-Home Generator?

Generators significantly impact sizing. A standby generator may consume hundreds of thousands of BTU per hour, more fuel during extended outages, and significant capacity in cold weather or long runtime events. A small tank may run a generator under normal testing conditions but become inadequate during a prolonged outage. That's why generator installs should include a fuel consumption review, tank sizing evaluation, regulator sizing, gas piping calculations, and delivery planning. The generator is only as reliable as the fuel system supporting it.

Aboveground vs. Underground Tanks

Location also influences the decision. Aboveground (AG) tanks offer easier access for inspection and monitoring, lower installation cost, and simpler maintenance — but they're visible and require proper placement clearances. Underground (UG) tanks preserve the property's aesthetics and protect the tank from some environmental exposure, but their considerations are more extensive: excavation, corrosion and cathodic protection, soil conditions, flood-zone requirements (your FEMA zone designation is a big deal in the world of gas tanks), and additional anchoring and inspection steps. Underground doesn't automatically mean better — it means the system has to be properly designed for the environment.

Planning for the Future Matters

A common mistake is sizing only for today, when many homeowners later add outdoor kitchens, fireplaces, pool heaters, generators, additional living spaces, or guest homes. A properly designed system considers future expansion — which is exactly why we built the Guardian Blueprint process for homeowners and builders who want a whole-home system but don't want to install all of it immediately. A slightly larger tank or properly sized infrastructure today can prevent expensive upgrades later.

A 2,500-square-foot home with a stove and fireplace may need a very different system than a 2,500-square-foot home with a tankless water heater, pool heater, outdoor kitchen, and standby generator. The best propane system is one designed around how you actually live.

How Do You Know What Size You Need?

A professional propane system evaluation considers total BTU demand, appliance usage patterns, generator requirements, regulator capacity, gas piping size, tank location, required clearances, local code, and future expansion plans. The goal isn't to install the largest tank possible — it's to install the right-sized fuel system. FAQs

Common Questions

What size propane tank do I need for a whole house?+

It depends on your total BTU demand, not just home size. A 500-gallon tank is the most common whole-home choice, handling multiple appliances, tankless water heaters, pool equipment, and a residential generator. Smaller homes with limited appliances may use 250 gallons; large homes, big pool heaters, or estates may need 1,000 gallons.

Is a bigger propane tank always better?+

No. Beyond holding enough fuel, a tank must supply enough vapor during peak demand. Vaporization depends on tank size, temperature, fill level, and appliance load. A tank can hold plenty of fuel yet struggle to keep up during high simultaneous demand, so it must be sized for both capacity and vaporization.

How does a generator affect propane tank size?+

Significantly. Standby generators can consume hundreds of thousands of BTU per hour and much more fuel during a multi-day outage or cold weather. A tank that's fine for normal use can fall short during a prolonged outage, so generator installs need a fuel consumption review, tank and regulator sizing, and delivery planning.
