Can My Propane Tank Supply All My Gas Appliances?

From your range and water heater to your outdoor kitchen and generator, learn what your propane system needs—and take home a free, simple planning sheet.

The short answer

It may. Before you add a range, tankless water heater, dryer, pool heater, fireplace, outdoor kitchen, or generator, have the whole gas system checked. The tank, regulators, and piping must work together to serve the appliances you plan to use.

Two questions matter: Can the system deliver enough gas at one time? And how long will the propane in the tank last? A tank can hold plenty of fuel and still be unable to supply gas fast enough.

1. Start with your appliance list

Write down every gas appliance connected to the tank, including anything you want to add. Find each appliance’s propane input rating on its label or in its manual. Look for BTU/hr, which tells you how much fuel energy it can use in one hour. Use the input rating, not the heat output.

Add those input ratings to find the total connected load. For example, a 40,000 BTU/hr water heater, a 60,000 BTU/hr range, and a 30,000 BTU/hr dryer add up to 130,000 BTU/hr. These are sample numbers; use your own labels.

This list starts the evaluation. It does not prove your current system can handle the load. Guardian Gas Solutions can check the information against the equipment and piping at your property.

2. Check the tank, regulators, and piping

Most home propane systems use vapor. The propane stored as liquid in the tank must turn into gas before appliances can use it. This is called vaporization. Tank size, fuel level, and temperature affect how quickly that happens. Lower fuel levels and colder conditions can reduce that supply.

Regulators control gas pressure. Piping carries the gas to each appliance. Their capacity matters, along with pipe length, fittings, and the pressure each appliance needs. Adding a larger tank alone may not solve a piping or regulator problem.

A system evaluation can identify whether the existing setup is suitable or whether a tank, regulator, or piping change is needed.

3. Estimate your propane use

For a simple fuel estimate, use about 91,500 BTU per gallon of liquid propane.

Gallons per hour = BTU/hr ÷ 91,500.
Gallons per minute = gallons per hour ÷ 60.
Gallons needed = gallons per hour × operating hours.

Example: An appliance using 91,500 BTU/hr burns about 1 gallon per hour, or 0.0167 gallon per minute, while firing at that rate. If it fires for 3 hours total, it uses about 3 gallons.

Many appliances cycle on and off. A water heater may not fire for every hour you are home, and a range does not always use every burner. For a daily fuel budget, estimate each appliance’s actual firing time, calculate its gallons, and add the results. Do not use a reduced daily average to size the gas system.

What changes when a generator is included?

A generator may run for many hours during an outage. Its fuel use changes with the electrical load. Use the propane gallons-per-hour figure for your exact model and expected load from the manufacturer’s fuel chart.

Do not treat the generator’s electrical kW rating as its fuel input. For gas supply sizing, follow its installation manual and required full-load fuel demand. Add the other gas appliances to the evaluation too.

If a model’s chart says 2 gallons per hour at your planned load, 12 operating hours would use about 24 gallons. That example covers only the generator. Cooking, hot water, and other gas uses need their own fuel allowance.

4. Estimate how long your fuel may last

On a home tank labeled in gallons, estimate the fuel now in the tank by multiplying its rated capacity by the gauge percentage written as a decimal. A 500-gallon tank at 60% holds roughly 300 gallons. The gauge is an estimate.

Subtract a planning reserve agreed with your propane supplier. If you set aside 100 gallons from that 300-gallon estimate, you would budget 200 gallons. The reserve is an example, not a universal refill rule.

At a steady combined use of 2 gallons per hour, 200 budgeted gallons would last about 100 operating hours. At 20 gallons per day, the same fuel budget would last about 10 days. Actual use and gas delivery capacity can change as conditions change. Plan deliveries before the tank runs out.
