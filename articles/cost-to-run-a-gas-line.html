How Much Does It Cost to Run a Gas Line to an Appliance?

A gas line isn't just a pipe — it's a fuel delivery system. The true cost depends on far more than feet of pipe. Here are the five questions that determine it.

One of the most common questions homeowners, builders, and contractors ask is: how much does it cost to run a gas line to an appliance? At first glance it sounds simple — 20 feet of pipe, so it should cost a set amount. But a properly designed and installed gas system isn't measured only in feet of pipe. The true cost depends on what's being installed, how the system is designed, what safety requirements apply, and what's between the gas source and the appliance.

A gas line is a fuel delivery system that has to safely move energy from the source to the appliance while maintaining proper pressure, capacity, ventilation, and code compliance. That requires evaluation before installation. Here are the five questions that actually determine the cost.

1. What Appliance Are You Supplying?

The first question isn't how far the appliance is — it's how much gas it requires. Every appliance has a BTU demand, and a small fire feature needs a fraction of what a tankless water heater, commercial range, pool heater, or standby generator does. As rough figures: a gas grill runs about 60,000–125,000 BTU, a fireplace 30,000–50,000, a tankless water heater 160,000–199,000, a pool heater around 399,900, and a residential generator often 200,000–600,000+ depending on size.

Appliance demand determines pipe sizing, required pressure, regulator configuration, meter or tank capacity, and whether the existing system can even support the addition. A line that's perfect for a grill may be completely inadequate for a generator.

2. What Is the Existing Gas System?

Before adding an appliance, the existing system has to be evaluated: Is the property on natural gas or propane? Is it a low-pressure (6–9 inches water column) or 2 PSI system? Is there enough capacity at the meter or tank? Are the regulators correctly installed? Is the piping sized for the added demand? Many homeowners assume they can just tap into an existing line. Sometimes they can — sometimes the system was never designed for the added load, and a professional evaluation prevents expensive corrections later.

3. How Far Is the Run — and What's In the Way?

Distance matters, but it's one piece of the puzzle. A 10-foot run can be more complicated than a 50-foot run depending on accessibility, wall penetrations, attic or crawlspace conditions, concrete cutting, trenching, landscaping restoration, and structural considerations. A proper install may involve pipe, fittings, shutoff valves, sediment traps, appliance connectors, regulators, pressure testing, leak testing, permitting, and inspection coordination. The pipe is only one part of the complete system.

4. What Materials Are Required?

Gas piping materials vary by application — black steel, CSST (corrugated stainless steel tubing), polyethylene (PE) underground pipe, copper where permitted, and stainless outdoor systems. An underground propane tank feeding multiple appliances may require proper burial depth, tracer wire, transition risers, corrosion protection, and tank separation. The correct material is based on system design, not simply what's cheapest to install.

5. Permits, Testing, and Code Compliance

A gas installation isn't complete when the pipe is connected. A complete professional install includes proper sizing calculations, code-compliant installation, pressure testing, leak testing, appliance connection verification, inspection, and documentation when required. Fuel gas systems are safety systems — the goal isn't just to get gas from point A to point B, it's to deliver energy safely for years.

So What Does It Usually Cost?

Every project is different. Many residential gas line installations range from several hundred dollars for a simple appliance connection to several thousand for larger, more complex systems. Costs go up with longer runs, higher-BTU appliances, underground work, new tanks, meter upgrades, multiple appliances, generator installs, commercial applications, and difficult access. The most accurate estimate comes from evaluating the complete system — not guessing from a distance measurement.

The better question isn't 'How much does it cost to run a gas line?' It's 'What does it take to safely and properly design a gas system that supports this appliance?' The cheapest installation isn't always the least expensive over time.

FAQs

Common Questions

How much does it cost to run a gas line to an appliance?+

Most residential gas line installations range from several hundred dollars for a simple connection to several thousand for larger or more complex systems. The real cost depends on appliance BTU demand, your existing system's capacity, the run distance and obstacles, materials, and permitting — not just feet of pipe.

Can I just tap into my existing gas line for a new appliance?+

Sometimes, but not always. The existing system has to be evaluated for capacity, pressure, and pipe sizing. A line that works for one appliance may be undersized for an additional high-demand appliance like a generator or tankless water heater. A professional evaluation prevents costly corrections.

Does a longer gas line always cost more?+

Not necessarily. A short 10-foot run through concrete or a finished wall can cost more than a longer, more accessible run. Accessibility, penetrations, trenching, and restoration often matter more than raw distance.
