How Long Will Propane Run Your Generator?

See the math. Compare LP and natural gas. Prepare for Florida’s signed maximum-load runtime disclosure.

Guardian Gas Solutions LLC · Published November 11, 2026 · Manufacturer data checked November 11, 2026

Florida update: effective November 17, 2026.

Rule 5J-20.052 calls for an estimated operating time at maximum generator load in consumer LP generator installation proposals, estimates, quotes and contracts, acknowledged by the consumer’s signature. It includes installations using a pre-existing tank. Read the adopted rule.

A shared article, video or calculator does not replace the job-specific disclosure and signature. The 80% starting fill and 20% planning reserve used below are stated assumptions, not percentages prescribed by this rule.

A 500-gallon tank is not 500 gallons of propane.

For these examples, start with the tank’s nameplate water capacity in US gallons and an assumed 80% propane fill. A 500-gallon tank begins with about 400 liquid gallons. Actual permitted filling depends on the container and filling conditions; a qualified supplier controls filling. Confirm the nameplate rather than relying on a sales nickname. A cylinder’s propane weight rating is not a water-gallon tank rating.

Starting LP gallons = tank water capacity × starting gauge % ÷ 100
Inventory-only hours = starting LP gallons ÷ full-load LP gallons/hour

Using Generac model 7327’s 3.95 gal/h full-load figure: 500 × 0.80 = 400 gallons; 400 ÷ 3.95 = 101.3 hours, or about 4.2 days of fuel inventory.

That is inventory arithmetic to theoretical zero—not promised operating time. A vapor-fed generator may lose adequate fuel delivery before all liquid is consumed. Temperature, remaining level, tank configuration, regulators and piping matter. Manufacturer maintenance and shutdown requirements still apply.

Plan a reserve separately.

For an illustrative 20% tank-level reserve, the 80%-to-20% window contains 300 gallons: 500 × (0.80 − 0.20) = 300; 300 ÷ 3.95 = 75.9 hours. This chosen reserve is not a guarantee that the tank can vaporize enough fuel, and it is not a universal Florida legal minimum.

Already have a tank? Use today’s level.

A 500-gallon tank reading 40% contains approximately 200 gallons—not 40% of the 400-gallon full-fill amount. Do not apply the 80% multiplier a second time. From 40% down to the example 20% reserve, 100 gallons remain for planning: 100 ÷ 3.95 = 25.3 hours. Gauges are approximate; arrange replenishment with your supplier before running out.

Show the fuel-input math without confusing it with electrical output

BTU/h describes fuel energy going into the generator. kW describes electrical power coming out. Multiplying kW by 3,412 gives the heat equivalent of electrical output; it does not give the generator’s fuel demand.

Educational LP conversion, on a matching heat-content basis:
LP gal/h ≈ propane input BTU/h ÷ 91,500 BTU/gal
Example: 366,000 ÷ 91,500 = 4.00 gal/h
500 × 0.80 ÷ 4.00 = 100 inventory-only hours

91,500 is a rounded educational energy-content assumption. If the manufacturer already supplies gal/h, use it. If it supplies LP vapor ft³/h, use that manufacturer’s conversion (Kohler and Cummins examples: 36.39 vapor ft³ per liquid gallon). Do not mix liquid gallons, vapor cubic feet, higher/lower heating values or fuel pressure with one another.

BTU and cubic feet: follow the units.

A cubic foot (ft³) measures gas volume. BTU measures energy; BTU/h measures the rate of energy use. Gas-volume figures refer to the manufacturer’s reference conditions, not the physical volume inside a pressurized pipe. Liquid gallons in a propane tank and cubic feet of propane vapor are different quantities.

LP vapor → BTU/h → liquid gallons

Use Kohler 26RCA’s stated full-load flow, heat factor and liquid-to-vapor conversion:

129 ft³/h × 2,500 BTU/ft³
= 322,500 BTU/h

322,500 BTU/h ÷ 2,500 BTU/ft³
= 129 ft³/h

129 ft³/h ÷ 36.39 ft³/gal
= 3.54493 liquid gal/h

For 72 hours: 129 × 72 = 9,288 ft³ of LP vapor, approximately 255.2 liquid gallons. This uses that manufacturer’s listed conversion factors.

NG cubic feet → BTU/h → therms

Use Generac 7327’s 316 ft³/h and an explicitly assumed 1,000 BTU/ft³:

316 ft³/h × 1,000 BTU/ft³
= 316,000 BTU/h

316,000 BTU/h ÷ 1,000 BTU/ft³
= 316 ft³/h

316 × 72 = 22,752 ft³
22,752 ÷ 100 = 227.52 CCF
22,752 × 1,000 ÷ 100,000
= 227.52 therms

Use the actual utility factor for a billing-energy estimate. A different heat-content basis changes BTU and therm values.

General relationship: BTU/h = gas ft³/h × BTU/ft³. Reverse it: ft³/h = BTU/h ÷ BTU/ft³. For LP liquid gallons, use the manufacturer’s vapor-volume-per-gallon factor. Do not apply the LP factor to NG.

Kohler’s nominal factors imply 36.39 × 2,500 = 90,975 BTU per liquid gallon. That differs slightly from the rounded educational 91,500 factor above. This is why we keep a calculation on one consistent source basis rather than forcing rounded or higher/lower-heating-value figures to match.

Natural gas: a continuing pipeline supply, with measurable fuel use.

LP: fuel stored at the property

Generac 7327 at full LP load: 3.95 gal/h.

Over 72 running hours: 3.95 × 72 = 284.4 gallons.

A 500-gallon tank starting at 80% has 400 gallons; the example 80%-to-20% planning window has 300 gallons.

NG: fuel delivered by utility

Same model at full NG load: 316 ft³/h.

Over 72 running hours: 316 × 72 = 22,752 ft³ = 227.52 CCF.

At an assumed 1,000 BTU/ft³, that is 227.52 therms. Use your utility’s actual heat-content/billing factor.

With normal, adequate pipeline service, a homeowner does not need to replenish an onsite NG tank. The practical questions are how much gas the generator uses each hour or day and what that usage adds to the bill. One CCF is 100 cubic feet; one therm is 100,000 BTU. At 1,036 BTU/ft³, the same 22,752 ft³ would be about 235.7 therms. Pipeline NG is not converted into a homeowner’s gallons of LP. Utility continuity, meter capacity, delivery pressure, piping and maintenance limit operation; do not promise unlimited runtime.

NG total ft³ = generator ft³/h × running hours
NG therms = total ft³ × utility BTU/ft³ ÷ 100,000

What will generator fuel cost per hour and per day?

Use the fuel rates for the actual model and load, and the price on your propane quote or natural-gas bill. No current local fuel price is assumed here. For NG, include the applicable per-unit supply and delivery charges in the rate you enter; check your bill for the unit and heat-content factor.

LP example: price per gallon

Hourly cost = LP gal/h × dollars/gal
Daily fuel = LP gal/h × 24
Daily cost = hourly cost × 24

Model 7327 at 3.95 gal/h uses 94.8 gallons per 24 running hours. At a hypothetical $3.00/gal, fuel costs $11.85/hour, $284.40/day, or $853.20 over 72 hours.

NG example: price per therm

Therms/h = ft³/h × BTU/ft³ ÷ 100,000
Hourly cost = therms/h × dollars/therm
Daily cost = hourly cost × 24

Model 7327 at 316 ft³/h uses 7,584 ft³/day. At 1,000 BTU/ft³, that is 75.84 therms/day. At a hypothetical $1.50/therm, fuel costs $4.74/hour, $113.76/day, or $341.28 over 72 hours.

If billed per CCF, divide hourly cubic feet by 100 and multiply by the dollars-per-CCF rate. At 1,036 BTU/ft³, 316 ft³/h equals 3.27376 therms/h; use that factor if applicable to your utility calculation. The sample prices above are arithmetic illustrations, not Florida quotes or a claim that one fuel is always cheaper. Each fuel’s full-load electrical output differs on this model.

These are variable fuel-use costs. Fixed monthly customer charges, taxes or surcharges not included in your entered rate, propane delivery fees, installation, oil, service and maintenance are additional. A standby rating and a 24-hour consumption example do not authorize operation beyond manufacturer limits.

Trades: update your process before November 17.

  1. Update every relevant proposal, estimate, quote and contract template. Provide a project-specific maximum-load operating-time estimate and a consumer signature acknowledgment, including where an existing tank will supply the new installation.
  2. Retain the evidence. Recommended records: exact generator/serial, fuel and maximum-load consumption source, verified tank capacity, assumed starting fill, reserve, other loads and system limitations. Recalculate when the equipment or tank changes.
  3. Confirm installation licensing. The rule identifies Category I dealers and Category V installers for LP generator installation.
  4. Document an arranged initial fill correctly. For the Category V/Category I arrangement described in the rule, disclose the Category I dealer’s name, address, phone and license in the consumer contract. Later purchases and deliveries are arranged directly with a Category I dealer.
  5. Explain the difference between fuel inventory and deliverable runtime. Review vaporization, regulators, piping, full-load fuel demand, shared appliances and maintenance. Show any half-load illustration separately; it does not replace the maximum-load disclosure.

This guide focuses on generator consumer safety under 5J-20.052. Other November 2026 chapter amendments also require review. It does not declare a blanket retrofit notice requirement for every existing generator owner, and it does not certify complete code compliance. Review the complete rule chapter.

Questions homeowners and businesses should bring to their installer

  • Which exact generator model and fuel specification are you using?
  • How many gallons are actually in my tank today, and what starting fill does the estimate assume?
  • How much remains above the chosen reserve at maximum generator load?
  • Will other gas appliances share the tank during an outage?
  • Can the tank vaporize enough fuel at the lowest expected level and temperature?
  • What maintenance interruptions and refill arrangements should I plan for?
