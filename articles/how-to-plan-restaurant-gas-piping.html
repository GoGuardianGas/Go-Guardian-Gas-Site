How to Plan Restaurant Gas Piping: 7 Steps to Code Compliance

A Florida restaurant owner’s guide to matching your appliance list, fuel supply, and kitchen layout before construction starts.

By Guardian Gas Solutions LLC · November 19, 2026

Before you order a fryer or commit to a cookline layout, confirm how gas will reach each appliance and who will sign off on the work. In a Florida restaurant buildout or remodel, a change as simple as moving a range can affect gas routing, hood coverage, and the inspection schedule.

Florida’s Building Code, Fuel Gas, and Florida Fire Prevention Code are starting points. This guide references the 8th editions in effect as of November 2026. Ask the city or county building department and local fire marshal which editions, amendments, permits, and inspections apply to your address.

1. Identify who reviews your project

Give the building department and fire marshal the address, suite number, and scope. Ask which gas, mechanical, and fire permits apply, whether a change of use is involved, and who can submit the drawings. If Florida DBPR licenses your establishment, check whether a separate food-service plan review is required. DBPR asks for a scaled plan with labeled equipment and a sample menu.

2. Build your appliance schedule

List each range, fryer, griddle, oven, and any other gas equipment on the system, including water heaters and rooftop units. Record the manufacturer, model, fuel type, and rated input in BTU/hr from the nameplate or spec sheet. Note inlet pressure requirements and mark future equipment separately. A used appliance’s fuel label and any manufacturer-approved conversion options are worth checking before you buy it.

3. Confirm the supply before reusing a line

For natural gas, ask the utility to verify available delivery pressure and meter capacity for the proposed load. For propane (LP), have your licensed installer and supplier confirm tank placement, delivery access, and whether the tank and regulators can supply peak demand. On either fuel, share the demand from existing equipment that will stay connected. An old meter or pipe tells you the space had gas service, but its capacity still needs to be verified.

4. Have the piping designed around the layout

Give your licensed gas contractor the scaled kitchen plan and spec sheets. Have the contractor document appliance loads, proposed routing, pipe lengths and sizes, regulators, and accessible shutoffs. The sizing depends on the connected demand, fuel, available pressure, allowable pressure drop, and piping route. Ask whether the permitting office requires a gas riser diagram or signed and sealed plans for your scope. Fuel Gas Code Chapter 4 covers these piping topics.

5. Coordinate the hood and fire suppression

Review the cookline together with the gas, hood, and fire-suppression contractors. Confirm hood coverage, makeup air, and how the suppression system will shut off fuel to protected cooking equipment when it activates. For movable gas appliances, have the contractor verify the listed connector, restraint, and approved equipment position. Review layout changes with the affected trades before moving equipment under the hood.

6. Schedule tests before concealing piping

Have your contractor obtain the required permits before installation and confirm the inspection sequence. Keep piping accessible until the inspector releases it for cover. Build time into the schedule for the required pressure test, any corrections, and the final gas and fire inspections. Coordinate gas service activation and appliance startup with your contractor and utility or propane supplier.

7. Close out the permits and hand off the system

Confirm all applicable building, fire, and licensing approvals before opening. Keep the approved plans, final inspection records, equipment manuals, and service contacts in one place. Have the installing team show managers where the shutoffs are and review the kitchen’s emergency procedures. If you swap or move an appliance later, check the gas capacity, hood coverage, and permit requirements first.
